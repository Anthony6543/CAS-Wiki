How detrimental could the effects of climate change be to Cyprus in the near future?

Cyprus, like many other coastal regions all over the world, is extremely vulnerable to climate issues. If nothing is done, then this will have detrimental impacts on many different sectors of the country, some examples being; The country's infrastructure, economy and natural ecosystems. If the world continues at the rate that the climate change is occurring, it could very likely cause the downfall of the entire nation of Cyprus. If Cyprus were to fall, it would cause a sort of chain reaction where many other countries fall with it. 

With Cyprus being a sort of ‘anomaly’ with their extremely strong economy but extreme vulnerability to climate change for it being a very small island, they will have an upper hand in fighting climate change over other small island nations. With this upper hand, they can develop more defences to push back the problems that arise from climate change, or even better, replace as much of their energy with renewable energy as possible. There is a problem however, which may compromise the economy by a huge amount if not careful. 

Cyprus is a small island loved by many people for many reasons, some of the reasons being the beautiful sights, the stunning beaches and the tropical weather. Most of Cyprus's income (about 80%) is from the “service sector”[^1]. The service sector includes all services that produce labour. Some examples of jobs that are part of the service sector are; Construction related jobs, for example a builder or an architect, healthcare related jobs, and other services, such as barber, painter and cleaner. In Cyprus however, A big chunk of the service sector comes from tourism. In Cyprus, the main tourist attractions are the stunning beaches there, with the crystal clear water and constant sun. By the year 2100, which is actually not as far away as you might think, all major beaches are predicted to be at least split in half, perhaps even eroded completely[^2]. This will be detrimental to Cyprus's economy because if most of its income collapses, then it will not have enough money to support itself, causing chaos and potentially many deaths there. Especially the people who live in the coastal areas that have to move homes because of these sea levels.  
   
	  
Climate change also poses significant risks to Cyprus’s trading economy, which as I mentioned before depends on agricultural exports and tourism revenues. It is quite common knowledge that climate change causes rising temperatures and changes in precipitation patterns. This can cause Cyprus to experience decreased agricultural efficiency, which will lead to reduced exporting of important products (I will go into more detail on the products that Cyprus exports later). Other problems that could emerge from this growing issue are extreme weather events, such as droughts, forest fires, heatwaves, storms and heavy rain. In the protected future, these events will be much more frequent and can become even more severe and cause even more issues.[^3]

This can affect supply chains and make it problematic for Cyprus to export goods on vulnerable routes that are used heavily By Cyprus. Almost all trading routes are in danger, as very few are sent by air with most in the water.[^4] The boats will be the most heavily affected by the effects of Climate change, for reasons such as extreme weather, change in ecosystem and efficiency of waterways. The only significant change for the air routes would be extreme weather, such as storms or heavy wind.

Even though Cyprus is a small island nation, Cyprus exports a huge amount of different products, these include; Petroleum refining, cement and asbestos-pipe manufacturing, and thermal electricity production are the republic’s heavy industries, and its light industries produce goods such as clothing, footwear, beverages, and some machinery and transport equipment.[^5] You might be surprised to see that Cypriot exports are about 3.6 billion euros, which is a lot considering how small the island is. According to the World Bank, about 15% of Cyprus’s GNI is from exporting and trading goods. With the huge damage that Climate change will bring, many of these farming and agricultural areas will be destroyed, losing a big chunk of that 15% of Cyprus's economy.[^6] This combined with the massive 80% of the GNI from the service sector (which was mainly tourism) will be 95% of Cyprus's GNI which is extremely vulnerable to climate change. To any country losing 95% of their GNI will be devastating. If this were to happen to Cyprus, the whole country would very likely collapse.

You may think that this problem is only relevant to Cyprus and other small, coastal nations, however there is also an issue that doesn't not just impact Cyprus and other coastal countries, it affects an entire union. Cyprus is a part of the European Union (EU), which means that Cyprus has to pay their part in funding for the EU. The money that Cyprus pays to them goes into all “EU funded missions”, some examples are; building roads, public services and medical equipment spread across all EU countries[^7]. Cyprus pays their “revenue” (how much they pay the EU) is about 250 million euros. This is almost 100 million euros more than the revenue of Malta, being about 150 million euros.[^8] If Cyprus were to lose a big portion of their economy due to the reasons mentioned before, then they will not be able to pay the EU what they are currently. This will affect EU missions and their overall function, which will cause a sort of chain reaction, making other countries have to pay more to make up for Cyprus. This will also give the EU a need to pay even more for protections against the effects of climate change, creating an even bigger financial hole for other countries to fill in.

After seeing all of these issues, Cyprus is making many efforts to reduce their climate emissions. In the year 2020, Cyprus polluted around 6771 kilotons of carbon dioxide. The last time that Cyprus had emitted a similar amount of carbon emissions was almost 26 years ago in 1998, where there was around 6,610 kilotons.[^9] This does not count the year 2013 because this year was an anomaly where they did not create much pollution at all, with only 6,427 kilotons. This was most likely due to the 2012-2013 financial crisis that Cyprus went through, pushing prices up by huge amounts and making people much less likely to consume when not needed, in return generating much less pollution.

Regarding the pollution per capita, that has also been on the decline since 2008 (excluding the years 2013-2015, where the public were still recovering from the financial crisis mentioned before). This may not seem like a very long time until I mention that the last time Cypriots have been recorded to have caused a similar amount of emissions was in 1990, where the average Cypriot emitted around 5.8 metric tonnes. In the year 2020, which is the latest date I have information to, the average Cypriot emitted around 5.5 metric tonnes.[^10] However this is not where it ends, this is because 1990 is not the most similar date to this figure, it is the earliest Information that I have. This means that emissions per capita now in Cyprus are at an all time low, since we started recording the emissions per capita back in 1990\. Another factor is now we are in the year 2024\. I am unsure why the world bank has not updated this statistic, but seeing the current downwards trend in emissions, we can see that 4 years after the information, it should be much lower than back then and matching a much earlier year.

Even with all of this improvement with the carbon emissions, this will not have a massive impact even if Cyprus got rid of their emissions entirely. This is because there are countries such as China and the USA which pollute thousands of times more than Cyprus. Now, obviously this is a very unfair comparison, of course Cyprus will have a much smaller amount of pollution as it is a fraction of the size of these other countries. This was only to show how Cyprus is almost powerless in terms of pollution and other countries influence it a lot more than Cyprus ever could. China’s pollution for example is 10,944,686 Kilotons of Co2[^11]. This is almost 11 million tonnes, 1,616 times more pollution than Cyprus emits. Even if Cyprus were to completely cut out their carbon emissions, this would barely have any effect on the overall emissions that affect everyone.

As an additional note, China’s pollution mainly goes to other countries as exporting. The information stated in the previous paragraph was solely to point out how small Cyprus is compared to the leading causes of the Carbon emissions. However even compared to the USA, who export much less than China, but also pollute much less at 4,320,533 Kilotons[^12], this is still 653 times more than Cyprus. As mentioned before, the USA is massive compared to Cyprus, with a much larger population (about 300x the population of Cyprus).

Taking all of this into consideration, Cyprus is not only cutting down on their CO2 emissions to combat climate change, they are also putting in other countermeasures to make sure the sea levels are kept ‘at bay’. One of these countermeasures are sea walls. Sea walls are hard, usually stone walls which break waves and prevent sliding of soil, causing less erosion.[^13] Massive sea walls are being constructed around the south-east coast of Cyprus, as that area is especially vulnerable to erosion due to the high winds in that area.[^14]

Another way that Cyprus is defending itself from the effects of climate change is Ecosystem-based adaptation (EbA for short). EbA involves using natural ecosystems, such as mangroves, wetlands, and coral reefs to defend the coastline and improve resilience to the impacts of climate change.[^15] Cyprus is researching the exploration and preservation of coastal ecosystems as a much more sustainable strategy to adapt to the outcomes of climate change.[^16]

	If Cyprus uses the technologies mentioned before and many others, it can improve its defences against the impacts of climate change, while (with EbA) promoting sustainable development and improving the quality of its coastlines. Both of the solutions mentioned are relatively cheap to do, which means that the government doesn’t really have an excuse why they shouldn't implement both of these, Eba is a bit more tricky and still needs some research, but it should be relatively easy in theory

	In summary, while the outcome of climate change on Cyprus is still uncertain. As a small coastal island, Cyprus is mainly vulnerable by its coasts eroding and destroying Cyprus’s flourishing economy. The threat that climate change poses creates a sense of urgency and a call to action to develop and improve ways for Cyprus to defend itself from the effects of climate change. As mentioned in the beginning, Cyprus does have an upper hand over other island nations with their strong economy and need to find ways to protect that strong economy from being damaged.  If Cyprus and other countries don't continue to add more countermeasures to combat these rising sea levels, we can ruin and potentially end many lives, not just in Cyprus but also in other coastal countries all around the world. Even with the current reduction in carbon emissions, it is still not enough to negate this issue. If a collective effort is made not just by Cyprus but the entire world, it is very possible to build a proper future for generations to come and bring an end to the coastal crisis.
